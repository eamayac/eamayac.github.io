<li>
  Safer Streets, Healthier Minds: Evidence from a Crime Prevention Program in Peru (with A. Aparicio Fenoll and Silvia Mendolia)
  <div class="paper-info"> <em>Revise and Resubmit</em>, <strong><em>Journal of Public Economics</em></strong> </div>
  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Aug 2026, Working paper <span class="paper-btn">[Abstract]</span> <a href="https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/w1y7edbktli2gkyep1qr0/crime_mental_Ainoa.pdf?rlkey=tafwk172blmhx34qiqcee0k6n&st=kiv014lp&dl=0">[PDF]</a></summary>
    <p> Can public safety interventions improve mental health? While a large literature shows that crime and perceived insecurity are associated with psychological distress, less is known about whether policy-induced improvements in local safety conditions translate into better mental health. We study this question using Peru’s Safe Neighborhood program, a large-scale crime prevention intervention that increased police patrolling in targeted urban neighborhoods. Exploiting the program’s staggered implementation across neighborhoods, we find that increased police presence improved mental health indices by about 0.13 standard deviations and reduced the incidence of moderate or severe depression. We document concurrent reductions in victimization, increases in perceived national police presence, and improvements in healthcare utilization and employment, suggesting that the welfare gains from public safety policies extend beyond crime reduction.</p>
  </details>
</li>


<li>
  When Extreme Weather Hits Home: Huaicos and Gender-Based Violence in Peru (with M. Tambussi) 
  <div class="paper-info"> <em>Submitted</em> </div>
  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Oct 2026, Working paper <span class="paper-btn">[Abstract]</span> <a href="https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/pa1w7sdr5bvwu1431nfrv/ipv_landslides.pdf?rlkey=p50rmbdahqsf3n4e43zsk9pu4&st=jby5apiu&dl=0">[PDF]</a></summary>
    <p> This paper examines how huaicos—climate-induced extreme weather events increasingly frequent in Andean countries—affect intimate partner violence (IPV). We link a database containing the geocodes of huaicos with the Peruvian Demographic and Health Survey for the 2010–2019 period. Using a sample of eventually treated women and exploiting the gradual and exogenous occurrence of these events, our results show that exposure to high-intensity huaicos increases both general and physical IPV by 21–26%. An event-study analysis reveals that these effects are persistent and intensify over time. Potential mechanisms include increased partners’ alcohol consumption, deteriorated women’s mental health, higher informal employment, and lower wages.</p>
  </details>
</li>


<li>
  Political Corruption Scandals and their Strain on Public Mental Health
  <div class="paper-info"> <em>Draft coming soon!</em> </div>
  <details class="paper-abstract">
    <summary>Aug 2026, Working paper <span class="paper-btn">[Abstract]</span></summary>
    <p> Beyond their direct impact on politics and the economy, corruption imposes significant societal costs on individuals and households, affecting their political beliefs and attitudes. In this study, I investigate whether political corruption scandals can also affect psychological well-being. I investigate how corruption scandals may harm citizens' mental health by taking advantage of major episodes involving some of Peru's most prominent politicians that occurred between 2013 and 2019. The identification strategy leverages the quasi-exogenous variation in individual exposure to these scandals, which is generated by differences in the interview dates of respondents in the Peruvian Demographic and Health Survey. I find that depressive symptoms increased in the immediate aftermath of these corruption scandals. These adverse effects persist at least in the medium run. I observe more pronounced effects when politicians who represent the system are involved, that is opposition and especially incumbent politicians at the national level, pointing towards political disillusionment as a likely mechanism. I conclude that the adverse consequences of corruption scandals extend well beyond immediate economic impacts and can even harm public health.</p>
  </details>
</li>
